# Planes of movement: nested frames of reference for structured animation (research, 2026-10-21)

Research note for the puppeteering programme (epic an#497; ADR 0008 decision 5, `misc/docs/adr/0008-*.md`; extends ADR 0007, `0007-scene-world.md`). Question: how do "inside the car vs outside the car vs the far background" generalise, how are such levels joined in movement, how do they interact, and how should `an` model it so the car is one instance and not a special case. The implementation issues are an#501 (frames), an#502 (reframe), an#503 (a plane at a world depth), an#504 (lagged coupling and sway), an#505 (the authoring skill), an#506 (previz). Beside this note, `planes_of_movement_frames.py` is a doctested reference implementation of the model (`python -m doctest planes_of_movement_frames.py`); every number below marked *measured* comes from it.

## 1. The failure, generalised

The car scene went wrong three times in one way. Near trees were baked into a rear plate that stood still while poles streamed past; the steering wheel changed size relative to Paul between framings; the receding road was 750 hand-eased screen-space actions that had to be re-mapped by script when a framing moved. Each is a thing placed in the wrong frame of reference, or in no frame at all: the trees belonged to the world and were drawn in the vehicle's plate; the wheel belonged to Paul's body and was placed per shot; the road belonged to the world and was animated in screen space, per shot. The scene-space work (ADR 0007, on branch `scene-space`) fixes the second and third by giving the scene one world and making shots viewpoints onto it, with a `travel` that moves the viewpoints and a `plates: {far, vehicle}` declaration for the first. Those are the right fixes, but `vehicle` and `far` are two named special cases of a general thing: a **tree of frames of reference**, each moving relative to its parent, with every element (a character, a prop, a plane, a camera) a member of exactly one frame at a time. The car is one instance: `world ⊃ vehicle ⊃ {seats, people, wheel, the cameras}`, `world ⊃ {road, poles, trees}`, and a `far` frame for the sky and the mountains. A ship's deck, a train window, a character on a moving platform, a crane-mounted camera, a prop picked up by a hand, a bottle thrown out of the window: all are the same tree with a different shape, and the last two are a member changing frame.

## 2. The idea is old: every "moving scene" trick in history is a frame change

- **The moving panorama (c. 1800 to 1900).** A long painted canvas wound between two rollers scrolled behind a stationary object, a boat or a horse, giving the sense of travel; Robert Fulton's 1799 French patent is credited with the spool design, and the rollers were hidden behind a cut-out proscenium [1]. The 1899 Broadway *Ben-Hur* ran real horses on separately rigged treadmills with a rolling panorama of the stadium behind them [2]. The actors are in one frame (the stage), the scenery is in another (the world, scrolled past), and the audience's eye is attached to the first.
- **The process shot and rear projection (1930s on).** Actors sit in a stationary car on a stage while previously shot moving footage is projected behind them; the practice was called process photography, and *The Wages of Fear*'s truck-cabin scenes are a textbook case [3]. The **Poor Man's Process** drops the screen: a plain background (sky), the car rocked on its suspension by grips, and "chasing lights", a row of lamps switched in sequence to mimic one passing light source [4,5]. Both separate the vehicle frame (cabin, cast, the lamp sweep as a *coupling* from the world to the cabin's lighting) from the world frame (the plate), and both fail exactly when something that belongs to the world is painted into the vehicle's layer, which is the baked-trees bug of the car scene.
- **The multiplane camera (1926 to 1937).** Lotte Reiniger's 1926 *Prince Achmed* used stacked layers under a fixed camera, so no parallax; Ub Iwerks built a horizontal multiplane around 1933; Disney's vertical camera, built by William Garity and first used on *The Old Mill* (1937), held up to seven planes of art on glass, each moved at its own speed to give depth, with some setups moving background and foreground in opposite directions for a rotation effect [6,7]. Fleischer's "setback" (1934, public debut 1935) is a different mechanism: cels in a vertical platen in front of a real forced-perspective miniature on a turntable, so the camera's lateral move showed true parallax of a 3D set behind 2D characters [8]. Yuri Norstein's stand places the camera above a metre-deep stack of glass planes, one every 25 to 30 cm, each able to move horizontally and toward or away from the lens, so a character approaches or recedes by its plane moving in depth [9]. In every case the cinematic "plane" is literally a frame of reference whose motion is set independently and whose apparent speed is set by its depth.

What the history gives the model: the elements of a scene are grouped into layers that move as rigid bodies; a layer's apparent motion is the sum of its own motion and the camera's, scaled by depth; and the oldest failure is a thing painted into the wrong layer.

## 3. The mathematics: a tree of frames, composed and projected

**A scene graph is a tree of transforms.** Each node's transform is relative to its parent, and an object's object-to-world transform is the product of the matrices along the path from the root [10]; this is the standard taught in every graphics course and the definition cited for the scene graph as such [11]. `an`'s own report 5 (§1.2) states it for the 2D stage as `world_C = world_A @ local_B @ local_C` with a pivot folded into each local matrix, and the runtime that draws `an`'s frames, PixiJS, stores the accumulated transform of every node in `worldTransform` and converts points with `toGlobal`/`toLocal` [12,13]; `an.stage.timeline.screen_position` is the Python twin (an#445). So the mechanism `an` needs for nested frames of movement is one it already runs at render time for the rig's bones and the environment's planes; what is missing is the *authoring-level* tree above the shot (a vehicle, a platform, a crane) and the lowering from it.

**Projection turns depth into apparent speed.** With the stage's axes extended by depth (x right, y down, z away), a point `p` seen from a camera at `c` with focal length `f` lands at `sx = f·(p − c)·right / depth` and an object of world size `S` is drawn at scale `(S/n)·f/depth` (ADR 0007's formula, `an/ir/space.py`). A camera translating by `d` therefore shifts a plane at depth `z` by `f·d/z` on screen, so **the parallax factor an engine stores is the lowered form of a plane at a world depth**: relative to the character plane at `z_ref` the factor is `z_ref/z_plane`; infinity lowers to 0, the character plane to 1, a nearer plane to more than 1 (*measured*: `parallax_factor(20, 2) = 0.1`, `parallax_factor(inf, 2) = 0`, `parallax_factor(1, 2) = 2`). This is exactly Godot's `Parallax2D.scroll_scale` convention that `an.stage.environments.Plane.depth` adopts, and the OpenClonk engine states the same inversion the other way round: a layer with parallax factor `Par` sits at distance `1/Par` from the camera [14,15]. Consequence for the model: a plane does not need a parallax factor *and* a depth; it needs a frame and, if that frame can move relative to the camera's, a depth from which the factor (for camera translation) and the streaming keys (for frame motion) are both derived.

**Relative motion between frames is Galilean.** For everyday speeds the coordinates of one frame in another are `x' = x − vt`, velocities add (`u_ground = u_train + v_train`), and a projectile thrown from a moving vehicle follows a different path for each observer [16]. A ball released from a moving car falls almost straight down *in the car's frame* and along a parabola that keeps the car's forward speed *in the world's*; the slight backward drift seen in real footage is air drag [16]. This is the rule for an element that **leaves** a moving frame: it keeps the world velocity it had, which is its own velocity plus its frame's (*measured*: a bottle thrown sideways at 3 m/s and upward at 2 m/s out of a car doing 20 m/s is, half a second after release, 2.1 m beside the car, 0.07 m above the seat line and still abreast of it, `z_rel = 0.5 m`; the common animation mistake, reframing it without the inherited velocity, puts it 9.5 m *behind* the car in the same half second).

**An accelerating frame applies a pseudo-force to its members.** In a frame accelerating at `a`, every member feels `−m·a` in addition to gravity [17]; a pendulum hung in a railroad car accelerating to the right hangs left of vertical, and the inertial observer outside explains the same tilt as the real horizontal force needed to accelerate the bob with the car [18]. In animation vocabulary this is follow-through, overlapping action and drag: the loose parts lag the main action and settle after it [19]. For a cut-out passenger it is the small torso lean of `atan(a/g)` away from the acceleration, the fuzzy dice swinging, the hair trailing; a derived channel of the frame's acceleration, not something an author should key per shot.

**The far frame couples by rotation only.** A thing at infinity (the sun, a mountain range, a cloud bank) never translates on screen however far the camera drives, but it swings across the frame when the camera *turns*. In frame terms: the far frame inherits its parent's rotation and discards its translation. That is a different coupling from "rigid", and it is why a plate drawn for a travelling shot may hold only what is at infinity (the owner's rule after round 2, now `plates: far` in ADR 0007, and the `depth` 0 of a parallax plane): the first thing in it that is *not* at infinity stands still while the streams move (*measured* from the same projection: a roadside pole 100 m ahead moves 30 px off-axis at t = 0 and 300 px at t = 4.5 s at 20 m/s; a mountain at infinity moves 0 px for any speed).

Three things recur across all of them. First, **the parent is a transform carrier, not a drawing**: Harmony's peg, AE's null, Blender's constraint target, tf2's frame. Second, **changing frame is an operation with a declared policy** about what is kept: world (the picture is continuous), local (the numbers are continuous), snap (the thing jumps to the target), and in physics engines also velocity (Godot's `platform_on_leave`, Unreal's weld). Third, **inheritance is per channel**: rotation, scale, translation can each be inherited or not, which is how a far plate (rotation only), a stabilised camera (translation only) and a billboard (translation and scale, no rotation) are all expressed in one mechanism.

## 5. The catalogue of couplings between frames

Each row says what joins two frames, what it prevents when declared, and how it lowers to what `an`'s stage draws.

| Coupling | Meaning | Prevents | Lowers to |
|---|---|---|---|
| **rigid** (default) | the child's pose is fixed in the parent's units | a passenger, a seat, a dashboard, a camera mounted in the cabin drifting apart; a prop attached to a body coming loose | one `stage` placement per shot (static) or keys from the composed pose when an ancestor moves relative to the viewpoint |
| **far** (rotation only) | the child turns with the parent and never translates | a sky or a mountain plate sliding under a travel; a baked near tree standing still | a parallax factor of 0 for camera translation; a shift of `f·tan(Δyaw)` for a turning viewpoint |
| **at depth** (a frame at a world depth) | a plane or a member in another frame, projected | the hand-tuned "0.6 factor" that fits one framing; a plate drawn for the two-shot pixelating in the close-up | a parallax factor `z_ref/z` for camera translation; sampled keys for motion of the frame itself (ADR 0007's `fit_channel`) |
| **lagged** (follow with damping) | the child follows the parent's pose through a first-order filter with a time constant | a camera that is welded to its subject (no feel), or hair and clothing that move as one block with the body | keys per frame from the filtered pose: exactly what `camera_follow` already emits (one linear key per frame, an#445), with a `lag` it does not yet have |
| **jointed** | the child's pose is constrained to the parent's at a point (a hand on a wheel, feet on a deck) with freedom elsewhere | hands leaving the wheel in a close-up, feet floating above a moving deck | the puppet solver's terms (puppet-research); the frame tree only says in which frame each joint anchor lives |
| **inherited velocity on leaving** | a member that leaves a frame keeps the frame's velocity added to its own | the thrown bottle that stops dead in the world and falls behind the car (*measured* above: 9.5 m behind after 0.5 s) | the reframe's initial velocity in the new frame; then keys from the new frame's own motion rule (ballistic, or authored) |
| **pseudo-force** | an accelerating frame tilts and drags its loose members | passengers sitting bolt upright through a braking or a turn | a derived lean channel `atan(a/g)` on a torso bone, a swing on hanging props; a solver term when puppet-research's gravity lands |
| **lighting sweep** | the world's lights pass through a frame (chasing lights) | an interior lit identically for a whole drive | out of scope for the frames model; the constraints catalogue's lighting row |

## 6. The general model

1. **Frames.** A scene's space has a tree of named frames; `world` is the root. A frame has a parent (default `world`), a pose relative to it (position `[x, y, z]` in the parent's units; a look as `[yaw, pitch, roll]`, where today's 2.5D stage needs yaw and roll), and a **coupling** to its parent: `rigid` or `far`. A frame has no art; it is a transform carrier (a peg, a null, a tf frame).
2. **Membership.** Every member of the space, every named viewpoint and every plane of an environment is **in** exactly one frame at a time (`in: <frame>`, default `world`); its position and size are in that frame's units. ADR 0007's `attached: {to: paul}` is the special case "in the frame of member paul", which the cut-out genre places from the rig; the rig's bones are the next level of the same tree.
3. **Motion of a frame** is authored like any member's: keys on its pose over film time (`set`/`tween` on `vehicle:at` and `vehicle:yaw`), or sugar for the common rules (`moving: {direction, speed}`, today's `travel`; `along: <path>` with a speed; `oscillate` for a deck's roll). The frame tree composes; the author never writes the sum.
4. **Viewpoints are members.** A shot's viewpoint is in a frame (`in: vehicle` for the windscreen two-shot and the side window; `in: world` for a roadside shot of the car passing; `in: crane` for a camera on a rig). The picture is the pinhole projection of every visible member's world pose into the viewpoint's world pose, per frame of film; the two-shot and the roadside shot are two viewpoints onto one world and they agree by construction.
5. **Lowering, as today.** The projection emits what the stage already understands: a single `stage` placement for a member whose pose relative to the viewpoint does not change in the shot; sampled keys (`fit_channel`, dense where `1/z` changes fast) for one whose relative pose changes because its frame, or the viewpoint's, moves; a parallax factor for a plane at a depth; nothing for a far plane. The compiled document, the renderers and the shot cache are untouched. ADR 0007's `_travel_entities` is this lowering for the one case `viewpoint in vehicle, streams in world`; generalising its `Projection.moved(by)` to `world_pose(viewpoint.frame, t)` and its stream instances to "members of any frame" is the whole implementation step.
6. **Reframe.** An action `{kind: reframe, target: bottle, to: world, at: 2.0}` moves a member to another frame at an instant with a policy: `keep: world` (default: the picture is continuous, the local pose is recomputed: Unity/Unreal/Godot's default), `keep: local` (the numbers are continuous, the picture jumps: `KeepRelative`), `snap: <anchor>` (the member jumps to a socket of the new frame: picking up a prop, `SnapToTarget`); and a velocity policy `inherit: velocity` (default when leaving a moving frame: the Galilean sum) or `inherit: none`. What happens after the reframe is the new frame's motion rule for that member: authored keys, or `ballistic` (closed-form, no dependency; the puppet solver's gravity when it lands is the same term integrated).
7. **Per-channel inheritance** is what makes `far`, "stabilised" and "billboard" one mechanism: a coupling is a set of inherited channels (`far` = `{yaw}`; `rigid` = all). v1 ships the two named couplings and keeps the set as the spelling underneath, so a third (`{x, z}` for a gimbal-stabilised camera) is an add.
8. **Lints** (deterministic, `an validate`): a frame cycle or an unknown parent (error); a plane under a viewpoint whose frame moves relative to the plane's, neither `far` nor at a depth (warning: today's `plates` check, generalised to any pair of frames); a member of a moving frame given a hand `stage.at` in a shot whose viewpoint is in another frame (warning: it will stand still while its frame moves); a `reframe` with `keep: local` whose world pose jumps by more than the cut-continuity tolerance (warning, with the jump in pixels); a `reframe` out of a moving frame with no `inherit` stated (info: say `velocity` or `none`); a `far` plane with a parallax factor other than 0 (error, already in ADR 0007); the screen-direction check across viewpoints in different frames (already in ADR 0007: the line is a property of the world). Whether a plate's *art* holds something nearer than infinity needs a vision model (cutan#95) and stays filed there.

**How it subsumes the special cases the owner named.** Inside the car, outside the car, the far background: members of `vehicle`, `world`, `far`. The body-attachment contract (cutan#101): a prop in the frame of a character, placed by the genre from the rig. The travelling view (an#484): `vehicle.moving` plus a viewpoint in `vehicle`; streams are generated members of `world`. The follow camera (an#445): a lagged coupling from the camera's frame to its target. A crowd (an#437): members generated into one frame. A ship's deck: a frame with an oscillating roll, its crew rigid in it, the horizon `far`. A train window: a viewpoint in `train` yawed 90°, the world streaming sideways from the same projection. A character on a moving platform: a member of `platform` with its own walk in platform units. A camera rig: `world ⊃ dolly ⊃ arm ⊃ camera`, each with its own keys.

## 7. What `an` has today, measured in code (main @ bc8639a6d; scene-space branch @ 956cec2)

*Naming note (added after the world PR, reeleehq/an#513, opened later the same night):* the persisted key is `world` (`SceneIR.world`, `World`, `WorldMember`), the lowering module `an.ir.world` (`lower_world`), the stage side `an.stage.world`, the CLI `an world show`; the review added slot entities with a `max_actions` budget for the travel, `overrides.attached` on added attached members and remapped finding paths. The names below are the branch's at the time of measurement; the proposal and the issues use the new ones.

- `an.stage.environments.Plane.depth` is a parallax factor applied to camera translation only, in the compile pass `parallax` (order 700); list order is draw order; no plane has a frame or a world depth.
- The 2D camera is `root.pivot`: named moves, keys (x, y, zoom, roll), `shake`, and `follow` (an#445), which emits one linear key per frame holding the target where it stood at the shot's start, with no lag parameter.
- `an.stage.timeline.screen_position`/`node_box` compose a node's point up its chain without a browser (an#445/467): the render-time frame tree exists for bones and planes.
- ADR 0007 (branch `scene-space`): `Space.members` at `[x, y, z]` with a size, `attached` members, named `viewpoints`, `Travel {direction, speed, near, far, streams, plates: {far, vehicle}}`; `an/ir/space.py` lowers a framed shot to placements and stream keys (`Projection.moved(by)` is "the vehicle moved"; `_travel_entities` samples `g(t)` with `fit_channel`); `an validate` checks the plates. Its "filed, not built" list already names three items this model covers: world-space motion and relative tweens, a plane at a world depth, a member fixed in the world under a travel.
- Nothing in `an` lets an element change frame, lets a viewpoint sit in any frame but the vehicle's (under a travel) or the world's (otherwise), or gives a plane a frame.

## 8. Design implications (ADR 0008 decision 5 and an#501 to an#506 record the schema)

1. Extend ADR 0007's `Space` with `frames`; give members, viewpoints, streams and planes an `in:`; make `travel` sugar for a moving `vehicle` frame plus a viewpoint in it; replace `plates: {far, vehicle}` by the planes' `in:` (with `far` a built-in frame).
2. One lowering, `world_pose(frame, t)` composed up the tree and projected into the viewpoint's world pose, generalising `_travel_entities`; static relative poses stay single placements, so a scene without moving frames compiles byte-identically.
3. A `reframe` action kind with the keep/snap/inherit policies; `ballistic` as the no-dependency motion rule after leaving a frame; the puppet solver's gravity as the later replacement at that seam.
4. The lints of §6.8 in `an validate`; the plate check becomes the general "plane in a frame that moves relative to the viewpoint".
5. The genre keeps what is its own: a character's frame is placed from its rig (body attachments), eyelines and views follow the viewpoint's frame; the production-pack skill tells writers to name the frames before placing anything (an#505).
6. The frame tree is independently reusable (tf2 is the proof that it is a package elsewhere) but has no second Python consumer in the fleet today; it goes in as one module in `an` (`an/ir/frames.py`, importing only the schema) marked as an extraction candidate, with previz (an#257) as the TypeScript twin that shares the vocabulary.
